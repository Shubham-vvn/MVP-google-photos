MVP PROBLEM STATEMENT
Google Photos — AI Memory Context

BACKGROUND

People capture hundreds or thousands of photos, videos, screenshots, and images every year.

However, when they need to find something months later, they often remember the EXPERIENCE behind the photo rather than the metadata associated with it.

They may remember:

“I met my old friend at a café in Delhi.”
“We had cake and talked about our childhood.”
“It was a really pleasant sunny day.”
“We walked towards Rajiv Chowk after leaving the café.”

But they may not remember:

- The exact date
- The exact location
- The café name
- The filename
- The album
- The month
- The exact words they originally searched for

This creates a fundamental gap between how humans remember experiences and how digital photo libraries traditionally organize information.

A photo library may know when and where a photo was captured, but the user's strongest memory may be the PURPOSE, CONTEXT, EMOTION, PEOPLE, ACTIVITY, or STORY behind that photo.

THE CORE PROBLEM

When people try to retrieve an old photo, they often search using the information they currently remember.

But the information they remember may not exist as searchable context in their photo library.

For example:

A user takes 10 photos and 3 videos while meeting an old friend at a café in Delhi.

Six months later, the user remembers:

“I met my old friend after several years. We went to a café, had cake, talked about childhood, and then walked towards Rajiv Chowk.”

The user may NOT remember:

- The exact date
- The café name
- The exact location
- Which photo was taken first
- What month it was
- What the file was called

The user's brain has retained the MEMORY OF THE EXPERIENCE, but the photo library has primarily retained the DIGITAL RECORD OF THE PHOTOS.

This creates a gap between:

HUMAN MEMORY
→ “What happened?”

and

PHOTO LIBRARY DATA
→ “When/where was this photo captured?”

The MVP should explore whether Google Photos can bridge this gap by allowing users to capture the meaning and context of their memories while that context is still fresh.

MVP HYPOTHESIS

If Google Photos captures a short natural-language memory/context summary from the user shortly after photos are captured, AI can transform that information into structured, searchable memory context and associate it with the relevant photos and videos.

Later, when the user searches using the memory they actually remember, Google Photos can use that contextual information to retrieve the relevant visual memories.

For example:

USER MEMORY:

“I met my friend Ramesh in a café in Delhi after two years. We talked about our childhood and had cake. It was a sunny day, and later we walked towards Rajiv Chowk.”

AI MEMORY REPRESENTATION:

People:
- Ramesh

Place:
- Café
- Delhi
- Rajiv Chowk

Activities:
- Meeting a friend
- Talking
- Eating cake
- Walking
- Taking the metro

Context:
- Meeting an old friend after two years
- Childhood memories
- Pleasant afternoon

Visual signals:
- Café
- Food/cake
- People
- Outdoor environment
- Metro/walking context

Memory keywords:
- Old friend
- Ramesh
- Delhi café
- Childhood
- Cake
- Sunny day
- Rajiv Chowk
- Catch-up

The AI should then associate this contextual representation with the relevant photos and videos.

THE MVP SOLUTION

Build an AI-powered Google Photos feature called:

“Add Memory Context”

The feature should allow Google Photos to periodically ask users to provide a short contextual description of recently captured or received visual memories.

The key principle is:

CAPTURE THE MEMORY WHILE IT IS FRESH.

The user should not be asked to manually organize every photo.

Instead, Google Photos should intelligently identify a meaningful group of recent visual memories and ask the user for a lightweight contextual input.

PRIMARY USE CASE — PHOTOS AND VIDEOS CAPTURED BY THE USER

This is Priority 1.

When a user captures a meaningful group of photos/videos during the day, Google Photos can identify the cluster.

For example:

10 photos + 3 videos captured during an outing.

Later that evening, at an appropriate time, Google Photos may show a lightweight notification:

“Remember this moment later?”

“Today you captured 10 photos and 3 videos from your afternoon. Want to add a little context so you can find these memories more easily later?”

Actions:

[Add Memory]

[Not Now]

[Don't Ask Again]

The experience should be completely optional.

If the user selects “Not Now”, Google Photos continues working normally.

If the user selects “Don't Ask Again”, this feature should stop prompting the user.

If the user selects “Add Memory”, the user is shown a simple conversational AI interface.

Example:

“Tell us a little about these photos.”

User:

“I met my friend Ramesh at a café in Delhi after two years. We talked about our childhood, had cake, and later walked towards Rajiv Chowk.”

The AI should then:

1. Understand the natural-language description.
2. Analyze the associated photos/videos.
3. Identify relevant visual entities and scenes.
4. Extract people, places, activities, objects, events, and contextual concepts.
5. Combine user-provided context with visual understanding.
6. Create a concise memory summary.
7. Generate useful semantic keywords/concepts.
8. Associate the memory context with the relevant photo/video cluster.
9. Where appropriate, associate specific concepts with individual photos/videos.

The user should see what the AI understood before saving.

Example:

“Here’s what I understood:

Ramesh • Delhi café • Old friends • Childhood memories • Cake • Sunny afternoon • Rajiv Chowk”

[Save Memory]

[Edit]

The AI should never silently invent personal facts.

User-provided information must be treated as the primary source of personal context.

AI-generated visual information should be clearly distinguishable from information provided by the user.

SECONDARY USE CASE — SCREENSHOTS AND RECEIVED IMAGES

This is Priority 2.

Screenshots and received images should not necessarily trigger the same frequency of prompts as personally captured photos.

Instead, Google Photos can wait until a meaningful group has accumulated.

For example:

8–10 screenshots collected over several days.

Google Photos may ask:

“You've saved several screenshots recently. Want to add a little context to help you find them later?”

The user could respond:

“Mostly screenshots of laptops and products I was comparing before buying a new laptop.”

The AI could create:

Purpose:
- Product research
- Laptop comparison

Objects:
- Laptop
- Specifications
- Product pages

Context:
- Purchase research

Search concepts:
- Laptop research
- New laptop
- Product comparison
- Specifications

This context can later help the user retrieve those screenshots using natural memory-based searches.

PERSONAL RECEIVED IMAGES

The MVP may also explore images received directly from individuals through messaging or social platforms.

For example:

A person sends the user several photos privately.

Google Photos can eventually associate the received images with lightweight context such as:

“Photos sent by Ramesh from our Delhi trip.”

However, this should be treated as a secondary scenario and should respect privacy and platform permissions.

The MVP should NOT assume that Google Photos can access private messages or private social-media content without explicit user permission and appropriate platform access.

CORE AI EXPERIENCE

The AI should behave less like a traditional search box and more like a memory-understanding layer.

The system should combine three sources of information:

1. USER MEMORY

What the user explicitly tells Google Photos.

Examples:

“I met my college friend.”

“We went to a café.”

“It was my first day at the new office.”

“I was comparing laptops.”

“We went on a family trip.”

2. VISUAL UNDERSTANDING

What AI can reasonably understand from the photos/videos.

Examples:

- People
- Objects
- Places/scenes
- Food
- Products
- Text
- Screens
- Documents
- Visual similarity
- Groups of related images

3. EXISTING PHOTO METADATA

Information already available to Google Photos, where permitted.

Examples:

- Capture date
- Approximate location
- Device metadata
- Existing albums
- Existing recognized entities
- Existing search/index information

The AI should combine these signals without allowing inferred information to overwrite explicit user-provided memory.

MEMORY → SEARCH

The ultimate purpose of this feature is retrieval.

Months later, the user may search:

“Photos from when I met Ramesh after two years.”

or:

“Photos where I went to that Delhi café with my old friend.”

or:

“That day we had cake and talked about childhood.”

or:

“Photos from the laptop research I did before buying my laptop.”

The system should use the previously captured memory context to retrieve the relevant photos/videos.

The user should NOT need to remember:

- Exact date
- Exact location
- Exact filename
- Album name
- Exact search keywords

The search experience should therefore move from:

“Search by what the photo is”

towards:

“Search by what the memory means to you.”

MVP USER JOURNEY

STEP 1 — CAPTURE

User takes photos/videos throughout the day.

Example:

10 photos + 3 videos.

STEP 2 — AI IDENTIFIES A POTENTIAL MEMORY CLUSTER

Google Photos recognizes that these photos/videos may represent a related event or activity.

STEP 3 — LIGHTWEIGHT PROMPT

At an appropriate time:

“Remember this moment later?”

“Add a little context to today's photos?”

[Add Memory]

[Not Now]

STEP 4 — USER PROVIDES NATURAL MEMORY

User writes or speaks naturally.

Example:

“I met Ramesh after two years at a café in Delhi. We talked about childhood and had cake.”

The input should support both:

- Text
- Voice

Voice input is especially important because the goal is to make memory capture extremely low effort.

STEP 5 — AI UNDERSTANDS

AI extracts:

People
Places
Activities
Objects
Events
Purpose
Context
Temporal references
Relationships
Semantic concepts

STEP 6 — AI CONNECTS MEMORY TO VISUALS

The system analyzes the related photos/videos and creates:

Memory summary
+
Semantic concepts
+
Photo/video associations

STEP 7 — USER CONFIRMS

Example:

“Memory saved”

“Ramesh • Delhi café • Childhood • Cake • Old friends”

[Done]

STEP 8 — FUTURE RETRIEVAL

Months later:

User searches:

“Ramesh café childhood”

or:

“the day I met my old friend after two years”

Google Photos retrieves the associated visual memories.

MVP SCOPE

The MVP should focus on one clear problem:

HELP USERS RETRIEVE PERSONALLY CAPTURED PHOTOS AND VIDEOS USING THE CONTEXTUAL MEMORY THEY REMEMBER LATER.

Priority order:

P0 / Core MVP:
- Photos captured by the user
- Videos captured by the user
- Grouped memories/events
- Natural-language memory input
- AI-generated memory representation
- Memory-to-photo association
- Memory-based retrieval

P1:
- Screenshots
- Documents
- Product research screenshots
- Other personally saved images

P2 / Future:
- Personally received images
- More advanced cross-app/contextual signals
- Automatic memory generation without user input
- Deeper conversational memory search

PRIVACY AND CONTROL

Because this feature deals with personal memories, privacy must be a first-class part of the MVP.

The user must control:

- Whether the feature is enabled
- Which memories are saved
- Whether AI-generated context is stored
- Whether a memory can be edited
- Whether a memory can be deleted
- Whether prompting is enabled
- How frequently Google Photos asks

The system should avoid creating unnecessary sensitive inferences.

The AI should clearly distinguish:

USER-PROVIDED CONTEXT
from
AI-INFERRED CONTEXT.

The MVP should not claim to know something simply because an image model inferred it.

DESIGN DIRECTION

The feature must look and feel like a native Google Photos capability rather than a separate AI application.

Use the existing Google Photos visual language and interaction patterns as the foundation.

The interface should feel:

- Familiar
- Minimal
- Calm
- Personal
- Trustworthy
- AI-powered without feeling overly technical

Do not create a generic futuristic AI dashboard.

The experience should feel like:

“Google Photos quietly helping me remember my own memories.”

AI should appear naturally through conversational prompts, intelligent suggestions, semantic chips, and contextual summaries.

The MVP should demonstrate a high-quality AI experience while maintaining the simplicity expected from Google Photos.

KEY PRODUCT PRINCIPLE

The feature is NOT designed to make users organize their photo library manually.

It is designed to capture a small amount of human memory at the right moment so that AI can do the organizational work.

The user provides the meaning.

AI provides the understanding and organization.

Google Photos provides the retrieval.

SUCCESS CRITERIA FOR THE MVP

The MVP should demonstrate that:

1. Users can easily add contextual memory to a group of photos/videos.
2. Users can express memories naturally rather than using predefined metadata.
3. AI can transform natural-language memory into useful searchable concepts.
4. AI can connect contextual information with the relevant photos/videos.
5. Users can later retrieve those photos using memory-based queries.
6. The interaction requires minimal manual effort.
7. The feature feels native to Google Photos.
8. Users remain in control of what memory context is saved.

THE CORE PRODUCT INSIGHT

People do not always remember photographs.

They remember moments.

They remember people.

They remember places.

They remember conversations.

They remember why something mattered.

They remember what happened around the photo.

The MVP explores whether Google Photos can capture that human context while it is still fresh and attach it to the visual memory before the details disappear.

The fundamental shift is:

FROM:

“Help me search my photos.”

TO:

“Help me find the photo using the memory I still have.”
